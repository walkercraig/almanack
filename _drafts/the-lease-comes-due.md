*Two hundred fifty years in, Americans still know how to take back what they lend. The harder test belongs to the people they just handed it to.*

By Craig Walker · Almanack

Kansas last sent a Democrat to the United States Senate in 1932. On Tuesday it sent Adam Hamilton. Texas had gone since 1988 without electing a Democratic senator, and James Talarico ended that streak by beating Ken Paxton. South Carolina, a state Donald Trump carried by nearly eighteen points two years ago, elected Annie Andrews, a pediatrician. Iowa put a Democrat in the Senate and another in the governor’s mansion on the same ballot. Greg Abbott, three terms in Austin, lost his job.

I write from Chicago, where a Democratic night surprises nobody. That is exactly why the map from the rest of the country gets my attention. When Wichita moves, something real has happened.

Chicago has its own long memory of these things, and some of it sits in plain sight. On the median at Wacker and Lake there is a cast stone marker with two brass plaques. One remembers the Sauganash Hotel, where the town held its board meetings when it was barely a town. The other remembers the Wigwam, the wooden hall thrown up on the same corner for the Republican convention of 1860, where Abraham Lincoln was nominated on May 18 of that year. The building is long gone. The traffic does not slow down for it. It is easy to stand at that light and never know that Lincoln’s road to Washington started a few steps away.

The count is still running, and the results are not certified. But the major news organizations have called control of both chambers. Come January, Democrats will hold the House and the Senate, and the President will face a Congress run entirely by the other party for the first time in either of his terms.

So the question worth asking in the country’s 250th year is what the voters actually said, and what they are owed now.

The cost of living and the war with Iran dominated the reckoning. Reuters and USA Today found Republicans losing ground in 99 percent of the roughly 2,100 counties with most of their votes counted by Wednesday morning. That is a whole country raising its hand at once.

The President spent his first public appearance after the returns touring the ballroom going up at the White House. “For me it’s relaxing,” he told reporters. His own Vice President was more useful. “The reason we lost is the war,” JD Vance wrote. Ron DeSantis called it “close to an extinction-level event for Republicans.” When your own bench is saying it out loud, the verdict is in.

Here is where the winners will be tempted to misread the room.

Look at the same state ballots. According to Ballotpedia’s early returns, which are still preliminary, Kansas elected Hamilton and approved a citizenship requirement for voting in its state constitution. Ohio sent Sherrod Brown back to the Senate, made Amy Acton governor, and approved a photo ID amendment. North Carolina elected Roy Cooper to the Senate and extended its photo ID rule to mail ballots. Arkansas, South Dakota, and West Virginia added citizenship requirements too.

Those results do not tell us how each person voted. They do tell us that electing Democrats and approving stricter voting requirements were compatible choices for these states. A Democratic victory is not a blank endorsement of everything Democrats want. The winners would be wise to notice the distinction before their consultants explain it away.

The country fired the management. It did not sign over the deed.

<p align="center">⁂</p>

Chicagoans know something about leases. We learned it the expensive way.

In December 2008 the city leased its 36,000 parking meters to a private company for 75 years in exchange for $1.157 billion. The details went public on a Tuesday. The City Council approved the deal two days later, 40 to 5. The next year the city’s own Inspector General concluded the meters were worth about $974 million more to the city than it got for them. The city and its advisers disputed that math, and they still can. What nobody disputes is the term. Seventy-five years. Every Chicagoan who feeds a meter on Clark Street is paying rent on a decision made in a matter of days, and the lease runs past the lifetimes of a good share of the people paying it.

That is the bad kind of lease. Too long, too fast, no exit. The kind where the people who signed it are gone and the people who pay for it never got a vote.

The framers were afraid of exactly that kind of arrangement, and they wrote the national lease short on purpose. The fifty-second paper of The Federalist, usually credited to Madison, walks through the history. The British Parliament had stretched its terms to seven years. Colonial Virginia elected its assembly about as rarely. The paper leans on an old maxim, that the greater the power, the shorter its duration ought to be, and it lands on two years for the House. The framers built the House on a two-year term for a reason. Dependence on the people requires returning to them. Two years, and then you come back and ask again.

Lincoln, the man nominated on that corner at Wacker and Lake, put it more plainly in his first inaugural. “This same people have wisely given their public servants but little power for mischief, and have with equal wisdom provided for the return of that little to their own hands at very short intervals.” Read it twice. Little power. Short intervals. Their own hands. That sentence is a lease with the terms printed right on it.

There is an older word for what the voters hand over, and Jefferson reached for it in a letter to Madison from Paris in September 1789, with the French Revolution about two months old around him. “The earth belongs in usufruct to the living,” he wrote. Usufruct is a lawyer’s word out of Roman property law. It means you may use the thing and eat its fruit. You may not wreck it. Someday it goes back. Jefferson was arguing about debt and whether one generation could bind the next. The word fits Tuesday better than he could have known. A majority in Congress holds the country in usufruct. It may plant. It may harvest. It may not salt the field on the way out.

Two years ago voters lent power to one party. On Tuesday they took it back and lent it to the other. The machinery worked. Tens of millions of ballots were cast and counted. The loser conceded in Texas. A sitting President said he would get along with the Democrats unless they turned destructive. At 250, that is an achievement worth recognizing: a people can lend power and call it back.

<p align="center">⁂</p>

Now the borrowers have to earn it. Chuck Schumer promised “legislative razor wire.” Fine. Razor wire is a fence. It does not make a week at the grocery store cheaper or end a war. Hakeem Jeffries says his majority will focus on affordability and accountability. Good. Hold him to the first word as hard as the second.

Accountability matters. Congress has obligations that do not disappear because a hearing might bore somebody. But an investigation is not an economic program, and a subpoena is not a household budget. A party that spends 2027 on cable hits while families keep paying more for less will have learned nothing from the people it just defeated.

Lincoln had advice for winners too. Two days after he won a second term, in the middle of a war, he stood at a White House window and told a crowd of serenaders to study the election’s incidents “as philosophy to learn wisdom from, and none of them as wrongs to be revenged.” He had more reason for revenge than anyone in Washington this week. He chose the other thing. The new majority can choose it too. The oversight that matters is the oversight that changes something for somebody. The rest is a grudge with a gavel.

Democrats will not control the presidency. They cannot honestly promise to govern as though they do. They can decide what legislation to put forward, what spending to authorize, what bargains to make, and which fights deserve the country’s time. They can show voters the difference between being obstructed and having nothing useful to propose.

If they spend two years confusing the two, they will hand this lease right back in 2028, and they will deserve to.

<p align="center">⁂</p>

Every Kansan who voted for Roger Marshall woke up Wednesday a full citizen. So did every South Carolinian who stuck with Darline Graham, and every Texan who voted for Greg Abbott. Their taxes are due on the same day. Their sons and daughters serve in the same war. Their claim on their government did not shrink when their candidate lost.

The first time the presidency passed from one party to the other, in 1801, Jefferson used his inaugural to say out loud what the winners owed. He called it a sacred principle: “that though the will of the majority is in all cases to prevail, that will to be rightful must be reasonable; that the minority possess their equal rights, which equal law must protect, and to violate would be oppression.” Rightful must be reasonable. Two hundred twenty-five years later, nobody has written the clause any tighter.

A majority that governs only for the people who voted for it is inviting the same correction that put it in office. The new one has two years to prove it learned something from watching.

That cuts the other way too. The people who lost on Tuesday owe the country what Paxton gave it: a concession and a return to the work of persuading neighbors. Contest a result through the lawful process when there is evidence to contest it. Otherwise, accept that other citizens get to win an election without having stolen the country.

The republic depends on that habit. Losing must leave you with rights, a voice, and another chance. Winning must leave you with limits. Neither condition is an inconvenience to be overcome. They are the arrangement.

<p align="center">⁂</p>

Lincoln said one more thing at that window in 1864. The election, he told the crowd, “has demonstrated that a people’s government can sustain a national election, in the midst of a great civil war.” He said that until then it had not been known to the world that this was possible. He was a little proud of it. He had a right to be.

Two hundred fifty years is a long time to keep that arrangement going. We have done it through worse than this. The voters did their part on Tuesday, in places that should humble anyone who claims to know this country from a single city.

I include my own city in that. The meters on Clark Street will keep collecting for decades no matter who wins anything. The lease on Washington came due Tuesday, right on schedule, the way the people who planned it wanted. On a median at Wacker and Lake, a brass plaque marks where the man who kept their arrangement alive through a civil war won his first nomination. The cars go by. The light changes. The arrangement holds, for now, because enough people keep choosing it.
